## ACT ONE — THE NIGHT (1:15–5:30)

**[VISUAL: Map graphic — Scotland, zoom to Orkney Islands, zoom to Scapa Flow. Animate the natural harbor shape.]**

**VO:**
To understand why this wreck is off-limits, you have to go back to October 1939 — five weeks into the Second World War — to a body of water called Scapa Flow.

Scapa Flow is a natural harbor in the Orkney Islands, ringed by land on almost every side. For decades it had been the Royal Navy's main fleet anchorage in the north — the safest place in Britain to keep a battleship. The entrances were narrow, the currents were vicious, and the Navy believed that made the anchorage effectively unreachable by enemy submarines.

**[VISUAL: Animated diagram of Scapa Flow's entrance channels, showing narrow gaps between islands.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
They were wrong.

**[VISUAL: Cut to a German U-boat silhouette, night, choppy water.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
On the night of October 13th, a German submarine — U-47, commanded by a 30-year-old officer named Günther Prien — threaded its way through one of those "impossible" gaps, running on the surface in near-total darkness, timing its approach to the tide.

Inside Scapa Flow, at anchor, sat HMS Royal Oak — a Revenge-class battleship built back in 1914, twenty-five years old by then, but still carrying over a thousand men.

**[VISUAL: Archival/stock footage of a WWI-era battleship if available, or animated cutaway diagram.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
In the early hours of October 14th, Prien fired his first spread of torpedoes. Most of the crew barely noticed — the explosion was small, and far from the ship's magazines. Many assumed it was an internal accident. No one on board yet believed they were under attack from a submarine that had somehow gotten inside their own harbor.

**[VISUAL: Slow zoom on an animated cross-section of the ship's hull.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
Prien turned his boat around, reloaded, and fired again.

This time there was no ambiguity. A second salvo struck the ship's side directly. Royal Oak began to capsize almost immediately — she rolled and went under in a matter of minutes, far too fast for most of the crew to escape.

**[VISUAL: Somber transition — black screen, single line of text.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
Eight hundred and thirty-three men and boys died that night. Some were as young as fourteen — boy sailors, part of the training complement still aboard. It remains one of the deadliest single losses in Royal Navy history.

**[VISUAL: Photos of survivors, rescue boats, if available — or respectful still-frame archival imagery.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
A nearby drifter, the *Daisy II*, managed to pull nearly 400 men from the freezing water. But for the majority of the crew, there was no time.

## ACT TWO — WHAT WAS LEFT BEHIND (5:30–9:00)

**[VISUAL: Underwater sonar scan of the wreck, slowly rotating 3D render if available, or stylized wireframe animation.]**

**VO:**
Royal Oak came to rest upside down in just over 30 meters of water — shallow enough that, in theory, any certified recreational diver could reach her. And for decades, some did.

Through the 1960s and 70s, before any legal protection existed, divers visited the wreck relatively freely. Most came out of curiosity, or reverence. But some came for something else — brass fittings, portholes, small artifacts that could be sold or kept as souvenirs. Pieces of a sunken warship, quietly leaving the site of a mass grave.

**[VISUAL: Close-up shots of salvaged brass items, museum display style, if footage available.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
Local authorities eventually stepped in with an informal diving restriction. But it wasn't until 1986 — nearly fifty years after the sinking — that the UK passed a law with real teeth: the Protection of Military Remains Act.

**[VISUAL: Text overlay — key excerpt paraphrased, not quoted verbatim, styled as a legal document graphic.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
The Act created two categories for military wrecks. A "protected place" — where divers can look, but can't touch anything or go inside the ship. And something far stricter: a "controlled site." At a controlled site, no diving is permitted at all, for any reason, without a license issued directly by the Ministry of Defence.

In 2002, HMS Royal Oak was formally designated a controlled site — the highest level of legal protection a wreck can receive in UK waters.

**[VISUAL: Map graphic showing the exclusion zone around the wreck site, marked in red.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
Today, a single green buoy marks the exact location. It carries no dramatic warning, no skull and crossbones — just a quiet, deliberate line: this is a grave. Stay away.

## ACT THREE — WHO IS ALLOWED DOWN THERE (9:00–13:00)

**[VISUAL: Archival or reenactment-style footage of Royal Navy divers preparing equipment.]**

**VO:**
The law isn't absolute. A small number of people are allowed near HMS Royal Oak — but almost none of them are civilians.

Since the 1980s, Royal Navy divers have made an annual trip to the wreck. Their task is simple, and largely ceremonial: they replace the White Ensign — the Royal Navy's flag — flying from the wreck, as a mark of respect for the men still aboard. It happens quietly, usually without press coverage, every single year.

**[VISUAL: If available, respectful archival footage of the ensign-changing ceremony, or a stylized reconstruction.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
Then there are the rare, licensed surveys — expeditions granted special permission by the Ministry of Defence, almost always tied to a specific research or memorial purpose.

The most significant of these happened between 2018 and 2019 — eighty years after the sinking. A team of professional divers, working with the Royal Navy's Northern Diving Group, the Royal Oak Association, and researchers from the University of Dundee and Newcastle University, was granted a special license to conduct a full survey of the wreck.

**[VISUAL: Sonar imagery, diver footage if licensed/available, or stylized recreation using the described survey findings.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
Their goal wasn't salvage. It was documentation — recording exactly what condition the ship was in, eight decades underwater, and gathering new detail on precisely how the torpedo strikes brought her down so fast. It's considered one of the most complete studies ever done of a sunken dreadnought-era battleship.

What they found, largely, was a ship remarkably intact. Royal Oak sank so quickly, and has been so undisturbed since, that researchers have called her one of the best-preserved battleships underwater anywhere in the world — a fully-armed warship, frozen in the exact condition she was in the moment she went down.

**[VISUAL: Slow reveal — full 3D wireframe/sonar render of the wreck, if available.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
Which brings us to the part of this story that never really ended.

## ACT FOUR — THE PART THAT'S STILL HAPPENING (13:00–16:30)

**[VISUAL: Close-up of water surface, subtle oil sheen visible if footage available, or stylized animated droplet rising from wreck to surface.]**

**VO:**
When Royal Oak went down in 1939, she was a converted coal-burning ship carrying around 75 separate fuel tanks — and she was fully fuelled at the time she sank.

Eighty-plus years later, some of that oil is still down there. And in small quantities, it's still finding its way to the surface.

**[VISUAL: Text overlay with the specific detail, cited plainly.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
It's not a dramatic slick — nothing like an oil spill in the news sense. It's a slow, persistent trickle, corrosion and pressure very gradually working oil out of tanks that were sealed nearly a century ago. Local boat operators in Scapa Flow have reported seeing it for decades. It's become, in a strange way, one of the quiet signs that mark the site even for people who'll never be allowed to see the wreck itself.

**[VISUAL: Slow pull-back to wide shot of the harbor, present day.]**

**VO (cont'd):**
No one has published a firm timeline for when — or if — that will stop. The tanks are corroding. Eventually, decades from now, they may simply run dry, or fail in a different, less gradual way. It's one of the open questions that periodic surveys like the 2018 expedition are quietly trying to help answer — not out of curiosity, but because if there's ever a larger release, it needs to be anticipated, not discovered by accident.

For now, the ship keeps almost all of her secrets to herself. A war grave, legally sealed off from the world, still slowly leaking evidence of the night she went down — to anyone patient enough to notice.
