# Octopus Mother Case File — Voice-Over Script

Oct 18, 2026 · @Annas Social

## 1. Cold open (about 1 minute)

*\[ON SCREEN: black. Low ambient sound. A single light moves through dark water.\]*

About fourteen hundred meters down, on a rock ledge in Monterey Canyon, a mother octopus is guarding her eggs.

Researchers find her. They note the spot. And they come back.

She hasn't moved.

They come back again. Months pass. Then a year. Then two. Then three.

She never leaves the eggs. No one ever sees her eat. And over time, her body visibly deteriorates.

*\[ON SCREEN: a counter running forward, "Month 1" to "Month 53".\]*

After more than four years, the researchers return one last time. The eggs have hatched. The mother is gone.

Her young were alive. She was not.

So what ended her?

There is no body to examine. But the clues were visible for years, and they point somewhere unexpected.

*\[TITLE CARD: CASE FILE — THE OCTOPUS MOTHER\]*

## 2. Case brief (about 1 minute)

*\[ON SCREEN: an evidence board. Five rating tags pin to it one by one.\]*

Most cases on this channel involve ships. This one involves an animal. The method does not change.

Every claim in this file gets one of five ratings.

Confirmed. Documented, and repeatable.

Probable. The evidence leans this way, but gaps remain.

Possible. Plausible, but unproven.

Unsupported. No evidence for it.

Disproven. The evidence says otherwise.

Our question is narrow. Why do octopus mothers decline and die after their eggs are laid? And is the mother on that ledge an exception, or the rule?

One warning before we start. This story has been told many times online. The more dramatic the version, the less likely it has been checked. We are going to check it.

## 3. The victim (about 2 minutes)

Start with what is solid.

An octopus mother lays her eggs and stays with them. She cleans them. She blows water across them to keep oxygen moving. She drives off anything that comes close. For most octopus species, this lasts weeks to a few months.

*\[RATING CARD: "Octopus mothers guard their eggs until they hatch." — CONFIRMED\]*

Rating: confirmed.

And in the species where it has been measured, brooding mothers eat little or nothing.

*\[RATING CARD: "Brooding mothers stop eating." — CONFIRMED (in species studied)\]*

Rating: confirmed, with one limit. We know it from the species that have been watched closely. Not all of them.

Now the extreme case.

The mother on that ledge belongs to a deep-sea species called Graneledone boreopacifica. Down there, the water is near freezing. Cold slows everything, including how fast an egg develops.

So she waited. Fifty-three months. About four and a half years.

That is the longest brooding period recorded for any animal.

*\[RATING CARD: "One deep-sea mother brooded for about 53 months." — CONFIRMED\]*

Rating: confirmed. Researchers returned to the same ledge again and again, and followed her across the whole span.

But hold on to this. She is the extreme, not the average. Most octopus mothers are never asked to wait four years. So the question becomes: is the end of her story the extreme version of something all of them share?

## 4. The suspects (about 3 minutes)

Four suspects. We take them one at a time.

**Suspect one: starvation.**

The mothers stop eating. Their bodies break down. This is visible, and it is consistent. Offered food, brooding mothers often refuse it.

*\[RATING CARD: "Starvation is how the mother dies." — PROBABLE\]*

Rating: probable. The body is clearly failing from lack of food. But that tells us how. It does not tell us why. Why would a healthy animal stop eating?

**Suspect two: the optic gland.**

Behind each eye sits a small gland. In octopuses it works like a command center for reproduction. It releases chemical signals that coordinate maturation, egg laying, and what comes after.

Hold that thought. We come back to it.

**Suspect three: evolution.**

Why would natural selection build an animal this way? There are several ideas. A dying mother does not compete with her young for food. She is not around to eat them. And all of her energy goes into one enormous reproductive effort, instead of being spread across many.

These are reasonable hypotheses. None has been tested directly.

*\[RATING CARD: "Dying after brooding is an evolved strategy that benefits the young." — POSSIBLE\]*

Rating: possible.

**Suspect four: choice.**

The most emotional version of this story says she decides to die. That she gives herself up for her young.

*\[RATING CARD: "She chooses to die." — UNSUPPORTED\]*

Octopuses are capable learners and problem solvers. But no study has shown that one can weigh its own death. The idea spreads because it is moving. It is not evidence.

Rating: unsupported.

That leaves two suspects standing. And one of them has been tested.

## 5. The 1977 experiment (about 2 minutes)

*\[ON SCREEN: lab-notebook aesthetic. Journal title card: "Science, 1977".\]*

In the 1970s, a researcher named Jerome Wodinsky ran an experiment that changed this case.

He took female octopuses that had laid their eggs. Then he removed their optic glands.

*\[ON SCREEN: two columns. "Gland intact" and "Gland removed".\]*

The females with intact glands behaved the way mothers do. They stopped eating. They declined. They died.

The females without the gland started eating again. They gained weight. And they lived months longer.

*\[RATING CARD: "Removing the optic glands extends a mother's life." — CONFIRMED (with limits)\]*

Rating: confirmed, with limits. This is an old study, in one shallow-water species, with small numbers. But later work points the same way.

So the gland sends the signal that shuts down feeding.

*\[RATING CARD: "The optic gland triggers the decline." — PROBABLE\]*

Rating: probable. The experiment is strong. But it is one experiment, and it does not show how the signal works.

Newer research has started to map that signal. A recent study traced part of it to the chemistry of cholesterol and steroid hormones. The picture is partial. But it points in the same direction.

Now, a myth worth removing. Some retellings say that without the gland, an octopus would live forever.

*\[RATING CARD: "Remove the gland and she is immortal." — DISPROVEN\]*

Rating: disproven. The females lived longer. They did not live forever.

## 6. The exceptions (about 1.5 minutes)

A good case file tests its own conclusion. So: does every octopus follow this script?

The general pattern first. Most octopus species breed once, and then die.

*\[RATING CARD: "Most octopus species breed once, then die." — CONFIRMED\]*

Rating: confirmed.

Males next. After mating, male octopuses also enter a decline. They eat less, lose coordination, and die within weeks to months.

*\[RATING CARD: "Males also decline and die after mating." — PROBABLE\]*

Rating: probable. It is well described in many species, but not in all.

Now the exception. A small Pacific striped octopus has been documented laying eggs more than once.

*\[RATING CARD: "All octopus mothers die after one brood." — DISPROVEN\]*

Rating: disproven. At least one species breaks the rule.

This matters. If the pattern were a law of octopus biology, there would be no exceptions. Instead it looks like a strategy that most species follow, and a few do not.

You will see this described online as "built to die." The evidence supports something narrower: a biological schedule that most octopuses follow, and a few do not.

## 7. Verdict (about 1.5 minutes)

*\[ON SCREEN: the evidence board again. A case-file stamp lands.\]*

Back to the ledge.

A mother guarded her eggs for four and a half years. She stopped eating. Her body wore down. And when her young were out, she was gone.

Who, or what, ended her?

Our finding: her own body. A signal from the optic glands, set off by reproduction, shuts down feeding and starts a decline she does not recover from.

*\[RATING CARD: "An internal hormonal signal ends the octopus mother's life." — PROBABLE\]*

Rating: probable. Not confirmed. The strongest evidence comes from a few species, and the full chemical chain is not yet mapped.

What remains open is why.

*\[RATING CARD: "It evolved because it benefits her young." — POSSIBLE\]*

Rating: possible. A reasonable idea, still untested.

And the claim that she chose it?

*\[RATING CARD: "She chose to die." — UNSUPPORTED\]*

Unsupported.

Nothing in the evidence points to a predator, an accident, or abandonment. What it does point to is a schedule written into her own biology.
